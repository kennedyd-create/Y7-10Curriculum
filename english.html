Purpose of English

Whaowhia te kete mātauranga.
Fill the basket of knowledge.

English equips students with knowledge of the codes and conventions of language and texts. Students learn to create their own texts with purpose and confidence and to appreciate classic and contemporary literature.

  • Students master foundational literacy and compose increasingly complex texts in a variety of forms.
  • They study global literary traditions and the bicultural and multicultural literary heritage of New Zealand.
  • As readers they become discerning textual critics; as authors they craft texts with clarity, creativity and control.

Years 9–10 prepare students for English, Media Studies, Journalism and Communications in Years 11–13.

Strands

Years 7–8 use three strands. Years 9–10 change to two strands that draw directly on them: Text Studies builds on Reading, and Language Studies builds on Writing and Oral Language.

Oral Language
Communicating and presenting, and communication for learning.
Reading
Reading enrichment, comprehension and critical analysis.
Writing
Transcription skills, composition and writing processes.
Text Studies (Y9–10)
Textual and critical analysis: features of text, context and purpose, interpretations and connections, response to texts.
Language Studies (Y9–10)
Crafting texts (audience and purpose, text types, literary essays, grammar) and oral communication.

Each strand is organised into elements and sub-elements. On this site each sub-element opens on its own.

Knowledge and practices

Knowledge sets out the content to teach and how deep to go at each year level. Practices set out the disciplinary skills and applications to teach. Both are teaching requirements, not achievement objectives.

  • They relate but are not an exact match. A practice can connect to more than one knowledge statement, and some knowledge has no matching practice.
  • The teacher decides the direction: start with the knowledge and use the practices to consolidate it, or start with a practice and make the links to the knowledge explicit.
  • Anything marked "e.g." is an illustration. You can substitute examples that better reflect the local environment, community, hapū and iwi connections, student interests or teacher expertise, as long as the intended knowledge and practices are taught.

Years 7–8 and 9–10

Phase 3 · Years 7–8

Explicit teaching of oral language, reading and writing moves students towards subject-English disciplinary knowledge. The focus is on components that take longer to develop, such as comprehension, vocabulary and oral language, while fluency and spelling are consolidated.

Students engage with increasingly complex texts, including authors representing New Zealand's bicultural and multicultural heritage, and learn how texts (including digital and media texts) represent people, places and ideas.

Phase 4 · Years 9–10

Integrated study of texts and language. Text Studies examines how texts reflect and respond to historical, cultural and social contexts, and how meaning shifts with perspective. Language Studies builds fluency and control across written, oral, visual and digital modes.

Students are introduced to literary tradition and learn to evaluate how digital and media texts are constructed and position audiences.

Text requirements

Phase 3 · Years 7–8

Each year students must engage meaningfully with:

  • at least one novel
  • a selection of poetry
  • a selection of non-fiction texts.

Teachers may also use film, drama, short stories, and visual, spoken, multimodal and digital texts.

Phase 4 · Years 9–10

Each year students must engage meaningfully with:

  • at least one novel
  • a selection of poetry
  • at least one film or drama text
  • at least one short story
  • at least one non-fiction text.

Teachers may also use visual, spoken, multimodal and digital texts.

In both phases, texts must be widely regarded as high quality and include seminal texts, texts by authors representing New Zealand's bicultural (Māori and Pākehā) and multicultural heritage, texts from around the world, and texts from popular and youth cultures. Students should also choose texts for personal interest and enjoyment.

Texts should vary in length and complexity: long, complex sentences, words with multiple meanings, visual features, layers of meaning that require inference, and ideas that invite critical thinking. Years 9–10 add multiple perspectives, complex plots and abstract ideas, and themes that challenge assumptions.

Structured literacy and English language learners

Structured literacy approaches are explicit, systematic and cumulative. In Years 7–8 most students have mastered phonemic awareness and phonics for decoding, and are consolidating fluency, handwriting and spelling. Teaching focuses more on the components that keep developing through life, such as vocabulary and comprehension.

  • Some students, including English language learners who join in Years 7–8, may still be working through a decoding sequence. Use diagnostic assessment and give intensive, targeted support with age-appropriate materials.
  • Use the English Language Learning Progressions (ELLP) and ELLP Pathway to plan language support, and explicitly teach academic and subject-specific language.
  • Support bilingual and multilingual students to use their first and heritage languages as resources for learning.
