Purpose of Science

Mā te whakaaro nui e hanga te whare; mā te mātauranga e whakaū.
By great thought the house is built; by knowledge it is made firm.

Science is the systematic, evidence-based discipline that builds knowledge through observation, prediction, testing and analysis. It fosters curiosity and gives students practical skills and ways of thinking they can use widely.

  • Students apply knowledge and scientific practices together to explain phenomena and solve problems.
  • They understand how people, ecosystems and resources are connected.
  • They use scientific knowledge to make informed decisions and engage with issues that affect communities and the world.

Years 9–10 prepare students for the Science subjects in Years 11–13.

Knowledge and practices

Knowledge sets out the content to teach and how deep to go at each year level. Practices set out the disciplinary skills and applications to teach. Both are teaching requirements, not achievement objectives.

  • They relate but are not an exact match. A practice can connect to more than one knowledge statement, and some knowledge has no matching practice.
  • The teacher decides the direction: start with the knowledge and use the practices to consolidate it, or start with a practice and make the links to the knowledge explicit.
  • Anything marked "e.g." is an illustration. You can substitute examples that better reflect the local environment, community, hapū and iwi connections, student interests or teacher expertise, as long as the intended knowledge and practices are taught.

On this site, a Same for Years 7 and 8 label means the document gives one statement that covers both years of the phase.

The practices

In Years 7–8 the practices are organised as Investigate, Evaluate, Communicate and Apply. In Years 9–10 the same practices remain but are written to align with the format of Year 11.

Five investigative methods are used: modelling, observing, classifying and identifying, pattern seeking, and fair testing (change one independent variable, measure one dependent variable, keep all others controlled).

Years 7–8 and 9–10

Phase 3 · Years 7–8

Students apply scientific practices with greater structure and precision: carrying out investigations, analysing and interpreting data, and building evidence-based explanations of more complex systems. They design and conduct fair tests and use theory-based models, find patterns in cell structures and explain ecological relationships using data and diagrams.

Phase 4 · Years 9–10

Students apply knowledge and practices in more abstract and interdisciplinary contexts. They carry out independent inquiry, critique claims with evidence, model systems, evaluate data quality and construct scientific arguments. They represent reactions with equations, calculate energy efficiency, analyse motion with Newton's laws, and evaluate human impacts on ecosystems.

Equipment

Phase 3 · Years 7–8

Materials: pure substances and mixtures (salt, sugar, sand, iron filings, copper sulfate, baking soda, vinegar); soluble and insoluble materials; conductive and insulating, magnetic and non-magnetic, transparent and refractive materials.

Tools: thermometers, multimeters, rulers, measuring cylinders, scales, timers; magnets, simple circuit set-ups, sieves, filters; notebooks, graph paper, tablets, cameras; spatulas, droppers, stirring rods, tweezers.

Equipment: beakers, test tubes, flasks, trays; goggles, gloves, aprons; torches, mirrors, prisms, lenses, tuning forks, sound meters; basic compound microscopes and prepared slides; molecular model kits and particle model diagrams.

Phase 4 · Years 9–10

Materials: copper wire, iron nails, zinc strips, sulfur powder, hydrochloric acid, sodium hydroxide, calcium carbonate; universal indicator, pH paper, Benedict's solution, iodine, hydrogen peroxide; hydrogen and oxygen (via chemical generation); preserved specimens, plant samples, yeast, pond water.

Tools: thermometers, multimeters, stopwatches, rulers, measuring cylinders, balances; conductivity and pH meters; notebooks, graph paper, tablets, cameras; spatulas, droppers, stirring rods, syringes.

Equipment: beakers, test tubes, flasks, Petri dishes, crucibles; goggles, gloves, aprons, fume hoods (if available); batteries, wires, bulbs, switches, diodes, resistors, motors, solar cells; lenses, prisms, sound meters; compound microscopes with prepared and wet-mount slides; molecular kits, circuit boards, anatomical models.

These are indicative materials, tools and equipment that support safe, evidence-based teaching of the sequence.
