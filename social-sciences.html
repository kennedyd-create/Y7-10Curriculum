Purpose of Social Sciences

He aha te mea nui o te ao? He tangata, he tangata, he tangata.
What is the greatest thing in the world? It is people, people, people. Meri Ngāroto, Te Aupōuri

Social Sciences is the study of how societies work and how people interact with each other and with their environments. In Years 7–10 it gives students the knowledge and skills to:

  • understand, take part in and contribute to local, national and global communities
  • think critically about social issues
  • judge how sustainable different social, economic, political and environmental practices are
  • engage respectfully with views and experiences that differ from their own.

Years 9–10 lead directly into the Social Sciences subjects students can take in Years 11–13.

The four strands

Civics and Society
How people belong, connect and live together. In Years 7–10 students move from local government and democratic values to New Zealand's system of government and international cooperation, building skills as informed, active citizens.
History
People, places and events over time, through New Zealand history (including Māori history and perspectives) and world history depth studies. Students use sources, compare interpretations and build evidence-based narratives and arguments.
Geography
Places, the processes that shape them, and why this matters to people. Students study migration, natural hazards, tourism, coasts, urbanisation and inequality, using maps, spatial tools and data to explain patterns.
Financial and Economic Activity
How money is earned, spent, shared, saved and invested, and how to weigh financial choices. From Year 9, students also learn about the New Zealand economy, markets and government budgets.

Knowledge and practices

Knowledge sets out the content to teach and how deep to go at each year level. Practices set out the disciplinary skills of social scientists. Both are teaching requirements, not achievement objectives.

  • They relate but are not an exact match. The teacher decides whether to start with the knowledge or with a practice.
  • Knowledge is organised around concepts. Each strand has a big idea across the phase, and each year has a conceptual knowledge statement that anchors the content under it.
  • Practices follow the same framing: Think conceptually, Frame questions, Process information, Identify values and perspectives, Communicate findings, and Design, implement and evaluate social action (not always used). Financial and Economic Activity uses only Think conceptually, Process information, and Identify values and perspectives.
  • In History and Geography, the Process information and Communicate findings examples must be taught. In all other practices, and throughout Civics and Financial, the examples can be swapped for others that meet the same intent.

Years 7–8 and Years 9–10

Phase 3 · Years 7–8

Students deepen their understanding of complex issues by analysing evidence, examining multiple perspectives and evaluating different interpretations. They refine their questioning and consider how values, beliefs and interests shape decisions. Key concepts include democracy, te Tiriti o Waitangi, migration, environmental change and financial systems. Social action becomes more deliberate: students identify issues, plan responses and consider how effective they are.

Te Tiriti o Waitangi: students examine He Whakaputanga and te Tiriti, their purpose, interpretations, and the perspectives of Māori and the Crown.

Phase 4 · Years 9–10

Students work as increasingly independent and critical social scientists. They critically analyse evidence, evaluate the reliability and bias of sources, and weigh different interpretations. They build sophisticated arguments, justify positions with evidence, and explore the ethical, social, environmental and economic implications of decisions and events through advocacy, debate and evaluating solutions.

Te Tiriti o Waitangi: students analyse its historical and contemporary impacts and explain its significance in New Zealand's constitutional arrangements, governance and society.

Across both phases students develop communication, collaboration, self-management, problem solving and creativity.

Local context

Teachers bring the sequence to life with local, visible examples: people and place, te ao Māori, and the lives of students and their communities.
